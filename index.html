SILHOUETTE, FABRIC, AND TWO CENTURIES OF CHANGE

The Western Dress

From corseted Victorian ball gowns to Chanel's little black dress, the Western dress has spent two hundred years rewriting what a woman's silhouette is allowed to look like. Here's how it happened, and the handful of shapes that still define almost everything hanging in a modern closet.

"Western dress" is really a shorthand — it covers the garment tradition that grew out of European court fashion and spread, through trade, colonization, film, and mass manufacturing, into the default wardrobe of much of the world. It isn't one dress but a family of silhouettes, each one a response to the fabric, technology, and social rules of its moment. Structured and rigid one decade, loose and liberated the next — the pattern repeats roughly every generation.

A SHORT HISTORY

Five Silhouettes That Changed Everything

1837–1901

The Structured Gown

Victorian fashion built the body as much as it dressed it. Corsets shaped the waist, and skirts ballooned outward first with layered petticoats, then with the hooped cage of the crinoline, and later the back-heavy bustle. A ball gown wasn't just clothing — cut, trim, and fabric quietly announced a family's wealth and standing at a single glance.

An early American ball gown, circa 1820, held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art — a precursor to the fuller, more structured silhouettes of the Victorian decades that followed. Image: Wikimedia Commons.
1920s

The Flapper Drops the Waist

After World War I, the corset largely disappeared and the silhouette went straight up and down. The flapper dress dropped its waistline to the hips, raised its hem to the knee, and let a woman actually move — dance, drive, work — in a way Victorian dress never allowed. It was less a fashion shift than a social one, worn by a generation of women who had just won the vote.

Actress Violet Romer in an early flapper-style dress. The straight, dropped-waist silhouette marked a sharp break from the corseted shapes of the previous century. Image: Library of Congress, via Wikimedia Commons.
1947

Dior's New Look

After years of wartime fabric rationing, Christian Dior's 1947 debut collection went the opposite direction: rounded shoulders, a tightly cinched waist, and a skirt that could use up to 15 yards of fabric. Some called it scandalously wasteful; Vogue called it a revolution. Either way, "the New Look" defined the feminine silhouette for most of the following decade.

Detail from Christian Dior's Fall-Winter 1947 "Zig-Zag" collection — the same year his debut "New Look" reintroduced a cinched waist and a full skirt after years of wartime austerity. Image: Wikimedia Commons.
1970s

The Wrap Dress Goes to Work

As more women entered the workforce, Diane von Furstenberg's jersey wrap dress became a symbol of a different kind of femininity: comfortable, self-fastening, and equally at home in an office or at dinner. Its front-crossing tie closure had roots stretching back to Orientalist-influenced robes of the 19th century, but von Furstenberg's synthetic-jersey version made it a modern, mass-market staple.

A modern wrap dress — the wrap-front silhouette popularized in the 1970s remains one of the most enduring shapes in Western daywear. Image: Wikimedia Commons.
1926 — ongoing

The Little Black Dress

Coco Chanel's 1926 design — simple, calf-length, unadorned — was nicknamed "Chanel's Ford" by Vogue, a nod to the Model T's promise of something well-made, affordable, and endlessly useful. A century later, the little black dress is less a specific garment than a category: one dark, simply cut dress every wardrobe is assumed to contain.

"Fashion fades, only style remains the same." — attributed to Coco Chanel
THE DRESS FAMILY TREE

Eight Silhouettes, Explained

Underneath the decades of trends, almost every Western dress on the market today is a variation on a small set of base silhouettes — defined by where the fabric is fitted, where it's released, and how it closes.

A-line
Fitted through the bodice, gradually widening toward the hem in the shape of a capital "A." Flattering on nearly every body type, which is why it has stayed a default choice since the 1950s.
Sheath
Cut close to the body from shoulder to hem, with little to no flare. Popularized in the 1950s–60s as a sleeker alternative to full-skirted styles; the classic "office" or "cocktail" dress shape.
Wrap
Closes by crossing one side of fabric over the other and tying or fastening at the waist, forming a V-neckline. Adjustable, forgiving, and famously popularized by Diane von Furstenberg in 1974.
Shift
A straight, unfitted cut that hangs from the shoulders without a defined waist — the 1960s mod uniform, echoing the loose lines of the 1920s flapper before it.
Ball gown
Fitted bodice, dramatically full skirt, floor length. The direct descendant of the Victorian evening gown, reserved today almost entirely for weddings and formal events.
Maxi
Any silhouette extended to ankle or floor length. Surged in the late 1960s and early '70s alongside bohemian and folk-inspired fashion movements.
Shirt dress
Built like a button-front shirt, extended into a dress length — collar, cuffs, and all. Borrows directly from menswear tailoring, a trend that runs through most of 20th-century women's fashion.
Fit-and-flare
Fitted at the waist, then released into a fuller skirt below — related to the A-line but with a more defined, cinched waistline, echoing Dior's 1947 New Look silhouette.
1926
The year Coco Chanel's little black dress was first published in Vogue.

HOW A DRESS IS BUILT

Cut, Dart, and Drape

Every one of these silhouettes comes down to a few construction choices repeated in different proportions. Darts — folded, stitched wedges of fabric — pull a flat piece of cloth in at the waist to follow the body's curve without extra seams. The bias cut, popularized by 1930s designer Madeleine Vionnet, turns the fabric 45 degrees so it stretches and skims the body rather than hanging stiffly — the technique behind most slinky, close-fitting evening dresses. And the number of seams and panels in a skirt determines how much it flares: a two-panel shift stays flat, while a gored skirt with six or eight panels can bell out dramatically without any gathering at all.

Fabric choice matters just as much as cut. Structured dresses — ball gowns, tailored sheaths — lean on wovens like taffeta, cotton poplin, or wool crepe, which hold a shape. Draped, body-skimming dresses use jersey, silk charmeuse, or other knits that move with the body instead of against it. Most silhouette shifts in fashion history line up with a change in available fabric technology as much as a change in taste.

CLOSING NOTE

Why the Dress Endures

A dress is the simplest possible answer to getting dressed — one garment instead of a coordinated top and bottom — which is part of why it has survived two centuries of upheaval in taste, technology, and gender roles more or less intact as a category, even as its silhouette has been rebuilt from scratch every few decades. Corsets came and went. Hemlines rose and fell by feet, not inches. But the basic idea — one piece of cloth, shaped to a body, worn as a complete outfit — has proven remarkably hard to improve on.
